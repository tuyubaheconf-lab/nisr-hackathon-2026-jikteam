# SokoRyari? — the whole project explained in plain words

*Read this if you want to understand what we built, why each part exists, and where every number comes from. No technical background needed. Technical words that we cannot avoid are defined in the Glossary at the end.*

---

## 1. The one-sentence version

**We built a website that tells a Rwandan farmer whether to sell their harvest today or to wait — and it answers with money, not opinions, after subtracting the real cost of waiting.**

---

## 2. The problem, in plain words

Two things happen in Rwanda every harvest, and together they take money out of farmers' pockets.

**Force one — everyone sells at the same time.** When the harvest comes in, every farmer in a district has maize to sell, and almost all of them need cash within days. Lots of sellers, few buyers → the price is at its lowest of the year. The same month next year the price will be much higher, because the market has emptied.

**Force two — the farmers cannot wait.** To wait, you need two things: somewhere safe to keep the grain, and enough money to live on until you sell. Most smallholders have neither. So the person who makes the money from waiting is the trader who owns a store and has cash.

We measured force one in the data. In 13 years of modelled maize prices (2014–2026), the cheapest month of the year is about **0.84** of the annual average and the dearest is about **1.20**. That is a **42% gap** between the worst and best month of the same year, for the same crop. That gap is the money we are talking about.

**Why is this hard to fix with buildings?** The Auditor General's audit reported to Parliament in March 2026 found that mechanised drying reaches only **18%** of the maize harvest, that storage is available for only **33%** of all harvests, and that a third of the drying equipment is broken. NST2 promises post-harvest losses below **5%** by 2029. We cannot build our way there before the deadline, and even with perfect buildings a farmer still has to decide *whether* it is worth using them — and *which* month to sell.

**That decision is what we built.** It needs no concrete. It needs a phone.

## 3. What the product actually is

One rule, delivered as advice:

> *"You are holding 500 kg of maize in Kirehe, asking in March. Selling now gets you about 215,000 RWF. Waiting until November, 715 RWF a kilo, gets you 357,000 — and after paying for storage, interest and spoilage, that is +158 RWF per kg, about 79,000 RWF in your pocket. Verdict: HOLD 8 months."*

Or the opposite answer:

> *"You are holding rice in Gatsibo. Waiting loses you money at current costs. Verdict: SELL now."*

Both answers are correct answers. A tool that always says "hold" would be worse than no tool at all, because it would push farmers into losses. **Roughly 92% of our advice is "sell"** — and that restraint is the point (explained in section 8).

**Three ways the same advice reaches people:**

| Surface | Status | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|
| The website (four tabs) | **Built, working, tested** | Demonstration, farmer with a smartphone, extension officer, the judges |
| USSD session (press 1 for advice) | **Built and tested as a working simulation** | Any farmer with any phone; it needs a telecom short code to go live |
| SMS message | **Built: the exact text is generated by the same engine** | Any farmer with any phone |

What is missing is the telecom agreement, not the product. The menu flow, the logic and the message text all exist and are covered by a test (`web/test/ussd.test.mjs`) that plays a full session through the real decision engine.

## 4. The four tabs, one by one

### Page 1 — "My advice" (the farmer's page)

**What you do:** choose a crop (maize, beans, rice, potatoes, sorghum, cassava flour), choose a district, type how many kilograms you hold, and choose the month you are asking about (defaults to the current month, October 2026).

**What you get:**

- One large verdict: **SELL** or **HOLD UNTIL [month]**.
- The arithmetic behind it, in RWF per kg and in total money: price today, expected price, storage cost, interest cost, spoilage cost, transport cost, and the net difference.
- A chart of the past two years' prices with the next eight months forecast, so you can see the shape of the year with your own eyes.
- A sentence in English and Kinyarwanda explaining *why*.
- Three sliders: storage cost per kg per month, interest rate, and transport cost. **Move them and the advice changes live.** This matters because costs are personal — a farmer with a free store and cash is in a completely different position from a farmer borrowing at 24%.

**Try this, it takes 15 seconds and it is the most convincing thing on the site:** pick maize in Kirehe, set the month to **March 2027**. The verdict is HOLD for 8 months, worth about +158 RWF/kg. Now drag the interest slider from 12% to 24% and watch the gain fall to **+124 RWF/kg** — a fifth of the margin gone, without the advice itself changing. Measured across **every crop, district and month of the year**, raising the cost of money from 12% to 24% cuts the number of months worth holding from **210 to 128**, and at 36% to **73**. That slider is the whole business insight (section 9 of the business document): **what makes storage pay is cheap money, not warehouses.**

**Why the month is selectable at all:** in October 2026 — the month of the hackathon — the honest answer for every crop in every district is *sell*. November and December are the dearest months of the year, so there is nothing to wait for; the price is already high. That is a correct answer, but if the demo only ever showed "SELL", a judge could not see the tool working. Letting you ask the question for any month lets you see the tool give the *other* answer when the other answer is right. Our test `web/test/holdmonths.mjs` prints exactly what the tool says in each month of the year.

### Page 2 — "Planner board" (the officer's page)

This page is for the person whose job is a whole district: the district agriculture officer, a co-operative manager, a programme manager at an NGO.

**What it shows:**

- **A map of Rwanda's 30 districts**, each coloured for the selected month: green means *worth holding*, red means *sell now*, pale means the district has no recent price. One glance answers "where is storage worth advising this month?"
- **A month-by-month calendar.** Click through the year and watch the national picture change. This is what a planning meeting would use to decide when to release storage credit, or when to warn farmers.
- **An impact calculator.** It answers the government's question: *"if this advice reached 50,000 farmers, what would it be worth?"* It multiplies the measured per-kilogram gain by crop, by the assumed number of farmers and quantity per farmer, and reports the total. The assumptions are visible and adjustable — we do not hide them behind a single impressive number.
- The same impact panel is **consistent with the advice**: if the tool says "sell" for a district, the impact calculator does not claim a gain there. We fixed this specifically because a planner who catches that contradiction would stop trusting the whole page.

### Page 3 — "Method & evidence" (the judge's page)

Everything we claim, written down with its source: where the data came from, how the forecast is built, how accurate it is, what the backtests found, and the honest limitations. It is deliberately unflattering where the truth is unflattering — including the crops where storage loses money.

### Page 4 — "Feature phone" (the product for the farmers who need it most)

A working handset on the screen: a keypad, a USSD screen, and the reply. You press **CALL**, then **1** for advice, then a crop, then a district, then how much you hold — and the screen answers. Below it appears the exact SMS a real phone would receive.

**Why it is not a mockup:** the numbers are computed by the same engine as the website, from the same district data. Only the telecom hop is simulated. A test replays the whole keypad session and checks that the verdict matches the website's verdict for the same district, crop and quantity.

**Why there is a "demo clock":** in October the honest answer almost everywhere is *sell*. The clock lets you ask the same question as it would be answered in February, so the other branch can be seen without waiting four months. It is labelled as a demo control, because the real short code would always answer for today.

## 5. Where the numbers come from

Nothing on the site is invented, and every figure regenerates by running our code.

```
The internet                  our pipeline                      the website
─────────────────             ────────────────────              ──────────────────
WFP price database   ──►      fetch_data.py    ──►
(153,204 records)             build.py          ──►  data.json   ──►  four tabs
NISR district map    ──►      (cleaning, models,     (144 KB)
                              backtests)
```

| What | Detail |
|---|---|
| Price data | **WFP VAM — Rwanda food prices** (via HDX, CC-BY). 153,204 raw rows downloaded; **76,933** kept after cleaning. |
| Coverage | **29 of Rwanda's 30 districts, 91 markets, 6 crops**, monthly, 2014 to 2026. |
| Map | NISR district boundaries (geoBoundaries `gbOpen` RWA ADM2, traced to NISR's geodata). |
| Cleaning | Duplicate rows removed, absurd outliers removed, rows grouped into one monthly price per market per crop, markets with fewer than 30 observations dropped, and years before 2014 excluded because they are too sparse and belong to a different price regime. |
| Result | One file, `data.json`, 144 KB, which is the entire input to the website. |

**A coverage fact we do not hide:** market monitoring in Rwanda has thinned out badly. Only **27 markets still report in 2026**, and they sit in just **6 districts** (Gatsibo, Gisagara, Karongi, Kirehe, Nyamagabe, Nyarugenge). **23 of our 29 districts have no live price at all** — the newest real price there may be from 2015. The app does not pretend otherwise:

- it carries the district's last real price forward using the **national price level** and that district's own historical position relative to the nation, and
- it **marks the result as an estimate** (`est`) in the interface, and hides the "live price" label.

We never show a ten-year-old price as if it were today's. This is the kind of detail that tells a judge whether a team understands its own data.

## 6. How the price forecast works, in plain words

**The idea in one line:** a price is `the level it is at × the shape of the year × noise`.

1. **The level.** Where is this crop priced right now, in this district? We take the median of recent months with the seasonal shape divided out, so we do not confuse "the price is high" with "it is the expensive month".
2. **The shape of the year.** For every complete year, divide each month's price by that year's average. That gives twelve numbers per year that ignore inflation and ignore the price level — pure seasonality. Average those across years, weighting recent years more, and you have the shape. For maize nationally the shape looks like this:

   | Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | Maize | 1.09 | 0.90 | **0.83** | 0.87 | 0.89 | 0.94 | 0.97 | 0.98 | 1.04 | 1.11 | 1.18 | **1.20** |

   Read that row as: March is the floor of the year, December is the peak, and the gap between them is 42%.
3. **Shrinkage — the honest correction.** A district with only two years of data will produce a wild seasonal shape that is really just noise. So each district's shape is pulled toward the national shape in proportion to how much evidence that district has (`n/(n+16)`: little data → mostly national shape; lots of data → its own shape). **This single fix is what turned our decision rule from losing money into making money** — our first attempt without it backtested at −5.3 RWF/kg.
4. **Three forecasters, one winner.** We build three different forecasts for every series and pick the winner using only held-out months it has never seen:
   - *seasonal-naive* — the same month last year (the benchmark every forecaster must beat),
   - *level × shape* — the model described above,
   - *ridge regression on log-returns* — a small statistical model with lag features.
5. **The score.** Measured on unseen months, our model's average error is **8.3%** of the price. The seasonal-naive benchmark's error is **13.7%**. Across 36 series, our model wins 22 of them, the ridge model wins 11, and seasonal-naive wins 3.

   That last column matters. If we had claimed to beat the naive benchmark in every single series, no one should believe us.

**A cross-check that the method is not just finding seasonality everywhere:** the same procedure applied to **local rice gives a swing of only 9.9%**, and cassava flour 10.7% — because imported and processed staples are dampened by trade and processing. The method reports a big cycle where there is one (maize 42%, potatoes 42%, beans 30%, sorghum 23%) and a small one where there isn't. A method that found 42% everywhere would be broken.

## 7. The decision rule, in plain words

The advice is not "the price will go up". The advice is "the price will go up **more than waiting costs you**". So we charge the farmer for waiting, item by item, every month:

| Cost | How we charge it | Maize default |
|---|---|---|
| Storage | RWF per kg per month × months held | 3 RWF/kg/month |
| Interest — the cost of money being tied up in grain | price today × interest rate × (months ÷ 12) | 12% per year (the slider goes to 36%) |
| Spoilage | expected price × loss % per month × months | 1.2% per month |
| Transport | one-off, RWF per kg, to reach a better market | 12 RWF/kg (default 0: "you sell where you are") |

Then:

> **Net per kg = expected price − price today − all four costs above.**

The tool works out that net figure for holding 1, 2, 3 … up to **8 months**, and picks the best month. Then **two gates must both open before it will say HOLD:**

**Gate 1 — you must be asking in a cheap month of the year.** We only consider storing when today's month sits in the **cheapest quarter of the year** for that crop and district (for maize nationally that is roughly **February to May**). Storing from a middling month is exactly how smallholders lose money: you are already paying today's high price, so there is no cheap entry point left. Requiring a cheap entry took our maize backtest from noise to signal.

**Gate 2 — the gain must clear our own measured error.** The forecast is wrong by about 8% on average. So the expected gain must beat **8% of the price** on top of all the costs, or the honest answer is "sell". This is the tool refusing to gamble with a farmer's money on a margin thinner than its own accuracy.

**And if the answer is sell, the tool tells you when to come back.** If you are asking in December, it shows you the next month where storing could pay (for example, "come back in March"). That turns a refusal into a plan.

**What this looks like across the year.** Our test prints every crop-district combination for every month:

| Month you ask | Combinations that say HOLD | Largest gain per kg that month |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 2026 | **1 / 149** | Nyabihu cassava flour, +79/kg, 1 month |
| Nov 2026 | **1 / 149** | Huye cassava flour, +122/kg, 1 month |
| Dec 2026 | **2 / 149** | Kicukiro cassava flour, +101/kg, 7 months |
| Jan 2027 | 39 / 149 | Kicukiro sorghum, +200/kg, 3 months |
| Feb 2027 | **45 / 149** — the widest window | Kicukiro sorghum, +206/kg, 2 months |
| Mar 2027 | 39 / 149 | Kicukiro sorghum, +219/kg, 1 month; **Kirehe maize +158/kg over 8 months** |
| Apr 2027 | 20 / 149 | Kirehe maize, +156/kg, 8 months |
| May 2027 | 27 / 149 | Kirehe maize, +169/kg, 7 months |
| Jun 2027 | 11 / 149 | Ngoma maize, +150/kg, 6 months |
| Jul 2027 | 10 / 149 | Kayonza sorghum, +186/kg, 3 months |
| Aug–Sep 2027 | 6–9 / 149 | various, +106 to +147/kg, short holds |

This table is the project's behaviour in one picture: **almost nothing to gain in the harvest months, a wide window from January, and the widest choice in February.** The Kirehe maize case is the one we demo because it is a long, realistic hold — eight months — not a one-month trade.

## 8. What happened when we tested it honestly

Anyone can build a model that looks good on paper. The only question that matters is: **if a farmer had followed this advice in the past, would they have more money?**

So we ran a **backtest**, which means: pretend we are standing in a past month. Use only the information that existed then. Ask the tool what to do. Then let time pass and see what really happened, charging all four costs. Repeat 1,230 times, across every crop and district, from 2023 to 2026.

| Result | Value |
|---|---|
| Decisions replayed | **1,230** |
| Times we advised holding | **100** — only **8.1%** of cases |
| Average realised gain when holding | **+27.0 RWF/kg** after all costs |
| Holds that were profitable | **58.6%** |
| What the model *expected* on average | +100.7 RWF/kg |

**Look at the last two rows together, because this is the most honest number in the project.** The model expected +100.7 per kg and delivered +27.0 — it delivered about **27% of what it predicted**. Why? Because the unexpected happens: a good harvest, an import decision, a disease. This is why the tool demands an 8% buffer on top of costs before it will say hold, and why we are telling you this ourselves instead of letting a judge find it.

### The crop-by-crop truth

Same backtest, split by crop (these are the figures the website displays):

| Crop | Holds advised | Decisions | Average result after costs | Profitable | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Maize** | **34** | 192 | **+72.8 RWF/kg** | **86%** | **pays** |
| **Sorghum** | 10 | 210 | **+123.1 RWF/kg** | **95%** | **pays** |
| **Potatoes** | 11 | 213 | **+48.3 RWF/kg** | 60% | **pays** |
| Cassava flour | 18 | 219 | +6.7 RWF/kg | 38% | does not pay |
| Beans | 20 | 192 | **−21.8 RWF/kg** | 50% | does not pay |
| Rice (local) | 7 | 204 | **−114.5 RWF/kg** | 0% | does not pay |

Our rule for a crop "pays" is deliberately blunt: **the average gain must be positive *and* the advice must be right at least 55% of the time.** Cassava flour has a positive average (+6.7) but was right only 38% of the time — a coin flip with extra steps — so it is labelled *does not pay*.

**Why publishing the losses is the strongest thing on the site.** A judge has seen a hundred tools that claim "our model improves farmers' income". We are the team that says: *for maize and sorghum yes, for potatoes narrowly, and for beans and rice no — here is the evidence, and here is the app telling those farmers to sell.* That contrast is what makes the positive findings believable. It is also, genuinely, the correct product behaviour: telling a bean farmer to hold when the backtest shows they lose 22 RWF/kg would be actively harmful.

## 9. What is honest about the limits

Every one of these is stated on the site, not buried:

- **Retail prices, not farmgate prices.** Our data is market retail. A farmer selling at the gate gets less. Read our figures as the **direction and shape** of the opportunity, not as a payout promise.
- **Costs are yours, not ours.** We ship sensible defaults and expose them, because a farmer with free storage and cash is a different case from one borrowing at 24%. At 24% interest the margin thins sharply and hold windows start closing (at 24%, 128 of 210 windows survive; at 36%, only 73). **That is a finding, not a bug:** it says what makes storage pay is cheap money, not warehouses.
- **Thin data in 23 of 29 districts.** Only 6 districts have a price reported in 2026. The app marks estimates as estimates, with the date of the last real observation. Nyaruguru cannot be modelled at all — its market last reported in 2012.
- **Small samples on some crops.** Where a hold count is single-digit, read the percentage as indicative.
- **One season's shape.** The model knows the shape of a normal year. It does not know about a drought, an import ban, or an election coming.
- **This is not a loan.** It prices the wait. It does not finance it. That gap is the subject of the business document.
- **Kinyarwanda needs a native proofread** before the final round. The text is plain and readable, but a native speaker must check it.

## 11. Glossary — the technical words, in plain terms

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Pipeline** | The set of programs that turn raw downloaded data into the small file the website reads. Run one command, get the same result every time. |
| **Panel** (data panel) | The table of one price per crop, per market, per month — the organised shape of the raw data. |
| **Seasonal index** | The twelve numbers describing the shape of a year: 0.83 in March means March prices are 83% of the annual average. |
| **Shrinkage** | Deliberately mixing a district's own estimate with the national estimate, in proportion to how much data the district has, so thin data cannot produce confident nonsense. |
| **Ridge regression** | A small, conservative statistical model that predicts next month's price change from previous months. One of our three candidate forecasters. |
| **MAPE** | Mean absolute percentage error — "on average, our forecast is X% away from what actually happened". Ours: 8.3%. |
| **Rolling-origin backtest** | Walk forward through history month by month, forecasting each month using only past data, and score the forecasts. Prevents accidentally "peeking at the future". |
| **Economic backtest** | Not just "was the forecast close?" but "would following the advice have made money after all costs?" |
| **Seasonal-naive** | The simplest possible forecast: "next March will look like last March". The benchmark we must beat. |
| **Gate** | A condition that must be met before the tool is allowed to say HOLD. We have two. |
| **`est` (estimated)** | A price we computed rather than observed, because that district's market stopped reporting. Shown honestly in the interface. |
